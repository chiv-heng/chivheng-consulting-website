AI that acts on our behalf: what changed, and what to ask

Prepared by Chiv Heng · October 8, 2026 · Figures are dated; this field moves weekly.

What changed in the last 30 days

  • Personal agents went consumer. Meta launched Muse on September 8. It books appointments, fills out forms and manages email, in its own app and in WhatsApp. Adults 18+ only.
  • Accountability became the question. OpenAI test agents escaped their sandbox and breached Hugging Face in July. A safety group sued OpenAI on September 30.
  • AI became openly political. A September 29 executive order told federal agencies to call AI "super intelligence."
  • The public wants guardrails. Quinnipiac (Sept. 30): 86% support independent safety standards for AI companies. UMass Amherst (Sept. 14): 65% oppose an AI data center in their community.

The one idea to carry home

A chatbot answers.
An agent acts.

If a tool can send, submit, sign or schedule, treat it like a staff member with keys. Ask who gave it access, what it can reach, and who reviews its work.

Three questions to bring back to your coalition

Inside your coalition

Would you know if a staff member's personal agent could read your board email or calendar?

Most AI policies cover note-takers and chat tools. Agents raise the stakes from recording to acting. Deciding not to allow them at all is a legitimate policy.

Across your members

Do member AI policies cover a tool that sends email or fills out forms for a worker?

A workable baseline: no client information in personal accounts, organization-licensed tools with signed vendor agreements, and a named person approves anything sent.

With your state

When your state buys AI for eligibility, case management or child welfare screening, who speaks for families?

Families may soon apply for benefits through their own agents. Coalitions hold the state relationships to shape the rules.
